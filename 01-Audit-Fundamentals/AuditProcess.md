# Audit Process

## Overview

The audit process is a systematic approach performed by independent auditors to obtain sufficient and appropriate audit evidence. The objective is to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

Understanding the audit process helps junior auditors recognize how each audit procedure contributes to the overall audit opinion.

# 1. Client Acceptance

## Objective

Before accepting an audit engagement, the audit firm evaluates whether it should accept or continue the engagement.

## Key Activities

- Evaluate management integrity
- Assess auditor independence
- Consider engagement risk
- Confirm competence and resources

## Output

- Engagement Acceptance
- Engagement Letter

---

# 2. Understanding the Business

## Objective

Understand the client's business, industry, operations, and internal environment.

## Examples

- Nature of business
- Business model
- Revenue sources
- Industry risks
- Internal controls

## Why It Matters

Without understanding the client's business, auditors cannot properly identify audit risks.

---

# 3. Risk Assessment

## Objective

Identify areas where material misstatements may occur.

## Examples

High-risk accounts include:

- Revenue
- Inventory
- Cash
- Accounts Receivable

## Output

Risk Assessment Documentation

---

# 4. Materiality

## Objective

Determine the threshold that could influence users' economic decisions.

Materiality helps auditors determine:

- Sample sizes
- Significant accounts
- Audit focus

---

# 5. Audit Planning

## Objective

Develop an effective audit strategy.

Planning includes:

- Team assignment
- Audit timetable
- Sampling strategy
- Audit programs

---

# 6. Test of Controls

## Objective

Evaluate whether internal controls are operating effectively.

Examples

- Approval controls
- Segregation of duties
- Access controls

If controls are effective, auditors may reduce substantive testing.

---

# 7. Substantive Testing

## Objective

Obtain direct evidence supporting account balances and transactions.

Common procedures

- Inspection
- Confirmation
- Observation
- Recalculation
- Analytical Procedures
- Inquiry

Examples

Cash

→ Bank Confirmation

Inventory

→ Stock Count Observation

Accounts Receivable

→ Customer Confirmation

---

# 8. Evaluate Audit Evidence

## Objective

Determine whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained.

Auditors evaluate

- Reliability
- Relevance
- Sufficiency

---

# 9. Completion Stage

Activities

- Review working papers
- Review subsequent events
- Obtain management representation letter
- Final analytical review

---

# 10. Audit Report

The auditor expresses one of the following opinions:

- Unmodified Opinion
- Qualified Opinion
- Adverse Opinion
- Disclaimer of Opinion

---

# Practical Example

A manufacturing company reports inventory of THB 50 million.

The auditor identifies inventory as a high-risk account because:

- Inventory is material.
- There is a risk of obsolete inventory.
- Inventory valuation requires management judgment.

Audit procedures may include:

- Observing the physical inventory count.
- Performing test counts.
- Reviewing inventory aging.
- Testing inventory valuation against the lower of cost and net realizable value (NRV).

---

# Key Takeaways

- Every audit begins with understanding the client.
- Risk assessment determines where auditors focus their work.
- Materiality influences the scope of testing.
- Audit evidence supports the audit opinion.
- The audit report is the final outcome of the audit process.
